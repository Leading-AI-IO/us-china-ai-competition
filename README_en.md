## 📖 Overview

Three numbers, presented without commentary. **74.24%** — China's share of the 131,121 AI patents granted worldwide in 2024. **51.91%** — the United States' share of forward citations to those patents. **14.31** — the figure for the country that leads in AI patents granted per 100,000 population, and that country is neither the United States nor China. It is South Korea.

These three did not come from separate studies. All appear in **the same Chapter 1** of the AI Index Report published by Stanford HAI in 2026 — compiled by the same institution, in the same year, from the same database. **Within a single indicator called "patents," changing the method of counting reverses the ranking three times.**

The question "who is winning the US-China AI race" is asked everywhere. But once those three numbers are placed side by side, the shape of the question breaks. When indicators diverge, arguing about victory conceals a prior choice: which indicator to adopt. **The conclusion is already contained in the selection of the metric.**

Yet something does not diverge even when indicators do: instruments. **This book reads instruments, not indicators.** China sets rare-earth output not as production but as an administrative **quota**, and stages its controlled elements in and out over time. The United States, for advanced semiconductors bound for China and Macau, imposes technical ceilings — TPP under 21,000 and total DRAM bandwidth under 6,500 GB/s — adds a licensing condition capping cumulative exports at 50% or less of domestic US shipment, and then extends its prohibitions to **the transfer of model weights trained on those chips** and **the provision of remote access to trained algorithms.** Regulation has reached past the object to what the object produces.

In the data layer, the received wisdom inverts. China chose not to fix ownership at all — separating rights into three — while the United States does have a federal data strategy. The asymmetry lies not in the presence of an institution but in its object. In the alliance layer, the US-led Pax Silica maintains a roster of 24 signatories, while the Chinese framework holds no roster of member states by design. The question "how many countries belong to both camps" therefore has **no answer**, not for lack of data but because the two forms are institutionally incommensurable.

Only the final layer resists being written into terms. Inbound movement of AI researchers and developers to the United States is down 89% from 2017 and down 80% in the past year alone; net inflow has fallen from 324.6 in 2022 to 26.0 in 2025. States can move regulation and resources. They cannot move people.

The central claim is one sentence. **What is being decided is not which side is stronger, but which side writes the terms the other must work under.**

This book also declines the layers it could not reach in primary sources: national shares of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, the composition of materials supply, national breakdowns of rare-earth refining capacity, and holdings of standard-essential patents specific to AI. Each bears on the subject, but only re-citations of paid market reports exist, or no public tabulation under a unified definition exists. **What could not be reached is not written as though it had been.**
